Overview

Metformin is a biguanide antidiabetic agent and the first-line pharmacological treatment for type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) in NZ and most international guidelines (BPAC NZ, ADA, NICE). It reduces blood glucose primarily by suppressing hepatic glucose output, with secondary effects of improving peripheral insulin sensitivity and reducing intestinal glucose absorption. Unlike sulfonylureas and insulin, metformin does not stimulate insulin secretion and therefore carries no intrinsic risk of hypoglycaemia as monotherapy.

Metformin is weight-neutral or associated with modest weight loss, making it a preferred agent in overweight patients. The UKPDS demonstrated significant reductions in macrovascular events in overweight T2DM patients treated with metformin, establishing its cardiovascular benefit independently of glycaemic control. It is also widely used off-label in polycystic ovarian syndrome (PCOS) to restore ovulatory cycles and improve insulin sensitivity.

⚡ Mechanism in brief: Activates AMPK via complex I inhibition → suppresses hepatic gluconeogenesis and glycogenolysis. No insulin secretagogue effect — no monotherapy hypoglycaemia.
▾ Detailed mechanism of action

Primary target — mitochondrial complex I: Metformin accumulates in hepatocytes (via OCT1 transporter) and inhibits complex I of the mitochondrial respiratory chain. This reduces mitochondrial ATP production and raises the AMP:ATP ratio, which activates AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK).

AMPK activation cascades: Activated AMPK phosphorylates and inactivates key gluconeogenic transcription factors (TORC2, HNF4α), reducing expression of PEPCK and G6Pase — the rate-limiting enzymes of gluconeogenesis. AMPK also inhibits glycogen synthase kinase, reducing hepatic glycogenolysis. Net result: 25–30% reduction in hepatic glucose output.

Peripheral effects: At skeletal muscle, metformin increases GLUT4 translocation to the plasma membrane, improving insulin-stimulated glucose uptake. It also reduces free fatty acid oxidation, which indirectly reduces hepatic acetyl-CoA availability for gluconeogenesis.

Gut effects: Emerging evidence suggests a significant GLP-1-mediated mechanism. Metformin inhibits bile acid reabsorption in the ileum, increasing intraluminal bile acids, which stimulate L-cell GLP-1 secretion. This contributes to post-prandial glucose lowering and appetite suppression. Metformin also alters gut microbiome composition, with increased abundance of Akkermansia muciniphila associated with improved metabolic parameters.

AMPK-independent mechanisms: Metformin inhibits mitochondrial glycerophosphate dehydrogenase, reducing the cytoplasmic NADH:NAD+ ratio and impairing lactate/glycerol gluconeogenesis. It also inhibits cAMP-mediated glucagon signalling in hepatocytes, blunting glucagon-stimulated glucose output.

Metformin is renally cleared unchanged (no hepatic metabolism). Its pharmacokinetic profile makes renal function the critical determinant of safety — accumulation in renal impairment is the primary mechanism underpinning lactic acidosis risk.

⚠️ Withhold before iodinated contrast: Hold metformin 48h prior to contrast administration in patients with eGFR <45 mL/min. Restart only once renal function confirmed stable post-procedure.
🚨 Lactic acidosis: Rare (<1 in 10,000 patient-years) but life-threatening. Risk is substantially elevated with eGFR <30, significant hepatic impairment, acute dehydration, or sepsis. Do not use in eGFR <30. Withhold during acute illness.

Adverse effects
Adverse effect profile
Severity Effect Action if it occurs
Common Nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea, abdominal discomfort — especially on initiation. Affects up to 30% of patients. Take with food. Slow dose titration. Switch to modified-release formulation. Usually resolves within 2–4 weeks.
Common Metallic taste, anorexia, reduced appetite. Reassure patient — usually transient. Take with food. Generally does not require dose adjustment.
Serious Lactic acidosis — rare (<1/10,000 patient-years) but potentially fatal. Presents with malaise, myalgia, respiratory distress, abdominal pain, hypothermia. Stop metformin immediately. Emergency medical assessment. IV sodium bicarbonate if pH <7.1. Haemodialysis to clear metformin. Do not rechallenge.
Serious Vitamin B12 deficiency — occurs in 5–10% of long-term users via impaired ileal absorption. May present as neuropathy or anaemia. Check B12 levels if symptomatic neuropathy or macrocytic anaemia. Supplement with IM or oral B12. Annual monitoring in long-term users is prudent.
Rare Hepatotoxicity — case reports of elevated transaminases and cholestatic jaundice. Check LFTs. Cease metformin if ALT/AST >3× ULN. Do not rechallenge. Consider alternative antidiabetic agent.
Rare Megaloblastic anaemia — secondary to B12 deficiency in long-term use. B12 supplementation. Review for other contributing factors. Haematology referral if severe.
Contraindications
Absolute contraindications
  • eGFR <30 mL/min/1.73m² — significant accumulation and lactic acidosis risk
  • Significant hepatic impairment (ALT/AST >3× ULN, Child-Pugh B/C)
  • Active or recent lactic acidosis
  • Acute decompensated heart failure with haemodynamic instability
  • Acute illness with dehydration, hypoxia, or haemodynamic compromise
  • Perioperative period with significant fasting (>24h) — withhold the morning of surgery
Cautions / relative contraindications
  • eGFR 30–60 — use with caution; reduce dose if eGFR 30–45; monitor 3-monthly
  • Iodinated contrast procedures — withhold 48h prior if eGFR <45; restart once renal function confirmed stable
  • Excessive alcohol consumption — impairs hepatic lactate clearance, increasing lactic acidosis risk
  • Elderly patients (>80 years) — renal function declines with age; monitor eGFR more frequently
  • Pregnancy — generally considered safe (off-label); risk-benefit discussion required
  • Concomitant nephrotoxic drugs (NSAIDs, aminoglycosides) — monitor eGFR closely

Drug or class Mechanism Severity Management
Iodinated contrast media Acute nephrotoxicity → metformin accumulation → lactic acidosis

Alcohol (excess) Impairs hepatic lactate clearance; combined risk of lactic acidosis
Advise against heavy alcohol use. Chronic excessive alcohol is a relative contraindication.
Cimetidine Competes for renal OCT2 secretion → ↑ metformin levels ~40%
Monitor for increased GI side effects. Consider switching to a different H2 antagonist (famotidine preferred).
Corticosteroids (systemic) Induce insulin resistance and gluconeogenesis — antagonise metformin's effect
Monitor BSL closely during steroid course. May require dose increase or addition of insulin for steroid-induced hyperglycaemia.
Insulin / sulfonylureas Additive glucose-lowering; insulin secretagogues independently cause hypoglycaemia
Metformin does not cause hypoglycaemia alone — combination risk is from the sulfonylurea/insulin. Counsel patients on recognition and management of hypoglycaemia.
Thiazide diuretics Raise blood glucose; diuresis may reduce renal perfusion and affect eGFR
Monitor glycaemic control and eGFR at initiation and after dose changes.
Counselling
✓ Take with food to reduce nausea and stomach upset — the most common side effects when starting.
✓ Your dose will be increased slowly over several weeks — this greatly reduces stomach side effects.
✓ Metformin does not cause low blood sugar on its own — you are not at risk of hypoglycaemia unless also taking insulin or a sulfonylurea.
✓ If you are having a CT scan with dye (contrast), tell the radiology team and your doctor that you take metformin — it may need to be held temporarily.
✓ Avoid heavy alcohol use while on metformin — it increases the risk of a rare but serious side effect called lactic acidosis.
✓ If you become unwell with vomiting, diarrhoea, or are unable to eat and drink — stop metformin temporarily and see your doctor or pharmacist.
✓ Long-term use can reduce vitamin B12 absorption. Let your doctor know if you develop tingling in your hands or feet, or unusual tiredness.
✓ Metformin works best as part of a healthy lifestyle — diet and exercise improve its effectiveness and may allow lower doses.
✓ If stomach side effects persist, ask your pharmacist about switching to the slow-release (modified-release) version — it is generally better tolerated.
✓ Take your tablets at the same time each day with meals for best routine and tolerability. Do not stop metformin without speaking to your doctor first.

NZ notes

Funding: Metformin is fully subsidised on the PHARMAC community schedule for the treatment of type 2 diabetes mellitus. No Special Authority is required. The standard-release formulation (500 mg, 850 mg tablets) is funded at full subsidy; the modified-release formulation (Glucophage XR 500 mg, 750 mg, 1000 mg) may have a patient co-payment — confirm on PHARMAC online schedule.

Scheduling: Prescription medicine (Rx) under the Medicines Act 1981. Not available as pharmacist-only supply.

Formulations available in NZ: Standard-release — 500 mg and 850 mg tablets. Modified-release — 500 mg, 750 mg, 1000 mg tablets (Glucophage XR). No injectable form available in NZ.

BPAC NZ guidance: Metformin is recommended as first-line pharmacological treatment in T2DM unless contraindicated. eGFR should be checked before initiating and at least annually thereafter (more frequently if eGFR 30–60 or if intercurrent illness). BPAC NZ advises that modified-release metformin may be offered when standard-release causes persistent GI side effects rather than abandoning therapy.

NZ practice note: Increasingly, add-on therapy with an SGLT-2 inhibitor (empagliflozin — Special Authority) or GLP-1 agonist (semaglutide — Special Authority) is considered when metformin alone is insufficient, particularly in patients with established cardiovascular disease or high CV risk per Heart Foundation NZ / NZF guidance.
